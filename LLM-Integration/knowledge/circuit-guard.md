# Circuit Guard — Hackathon Winner

Circuit Guard won the Ampere Track (AI for Motorsport Safety) at FormulaTechHacks, held 26–28 September 2026. Rayyan Moosani was the team lead of the four-person team. This is his flagship project. Each section below is self-contained.

## What is Circuit Guard?
Circuit Guard is an AI motorsport-safety platform that Rayyan Moosani led a team of four to build at FormulaTechHacks in September 2026, where it won the Ampere Track (AI for Motorsport Safety). Rayyan was the team lead. It detects crashes and lost grip in live Formula 1 telemetry, warns the cars behind, recommends the steward's call with cited FIA regulations, and turns the same data into evidence a circuit can give its insurer. In one line: an AI race-control and insurance platform that spots danger during the race, finds the corners that keep hurting people, and gives insurers evidence instead of a guess.

## Did Circuit Guard win anything?
Yes. Circuit Guard won the Ampere Track — the AI for Motorsport Safety track — at FormulaTechHacks (26–28 September 2026), with Rayyan Moosani leading the four-person team. The track asked teams to build an AI solution that detects, predicts or prevents safety risks in motorsport; Circuit Guard does all three: it detects incidents live, predicts the risky corners from history, and prevents harm with warnings, steward calls and a low-cost safety plan.

## What problem does Circuit Guard solve?
Every crash in Formula 1 puts marshals and fans at risk, not only the driver — marshals walk onto a live track to clear a stopped car, and fans sit metres away. Today race control, circuits and insurers each handle that risk separately and each is missing information. Race control reacts to each crash under time pressure and often learns about a slippery corner from the first car that crashes there. Circuits cannot say which corners keep endangering people, so insurers price the uncertainty; in September 2025 the FIA set up a global task force on rising motorsport insurance costs. Circuit Guard, Rayyan Moosani's award-winning project, connects race control, circuits and insurers on one live map.

## What inspired Circuit Guard?
Two real events shaped Circuit Guard, Rayyan Moosani's FormulaTechHacks-winning project. At the 2013 Canadian Grand Prix, volunteer marshal Mark Robinson was killed by a recovery vehicle while a crashed car was being removed. At the 2007 European Grand Prix at the Nürburgring, a sudden downpour sent car after car off at the same corner on the first lap. Both come down to one moment: a car stops and people must go out to it. Circuit Guard is built for exactly that moment.

## What does Circuit Guard do during a race?
During a race, Circuit Guard runs a live safety loop. It replays real incidents from OpenF1 telemetry on a 2D or 3D map with every car at its real position. Detectors flag stopped cars, contact, and cars braking far below what their tyres should give. Marshal light panels change sector by sector (yellow, double yellow, slippery surface, VSC, Safety Car, red) at the real marshal-post positions, the car behind gets a cockpit warning with the distance to the incident, and a Claude steward agent writes a steward card with the recommended flag or Safety Car and cited FIA 2026 Sporting Regulations. Race control always makes the final decision. Rayyan Moosani, the team lead, built the unified race-control map and integrated the team's detectors into this live loop.

## How does the Claude steward agent work?
Rayyan Moosani, Circuit Guard's team lead, integrated a Claude steward agent into the live loop. It receives only facts computed from real telemetry plus FIA regulation articles retrieved with MiniLM sentence embeddings, and returns a schema-constrained card: a headline, the recommended action, reasoning with article citations, a message per car behind, steps for race control and marshals, and spectator safety notes. The output is validated with structured outputs, any citation not in the retrieved set is dropped, and a deterministic rule engine takes over if Claude is unavailable — so the AI steward stays grounded and never invents a rule.

## What is the wet-hairpin demo?
The wet-hairpin demo, built by team lead Rayyan Moosani, sends a simulated aquaplaning crash through Circuit Guard's real safety loop at the Montreal hairpin, modelled on the 2007 Nürburgring downpour and clearly labelled as simulated. A 240 Hz vehicle-physics model sends car A into standing water, snaps it sideways and slides it into the tyre wall. Race control flags it at once and warns car B, which brakes early, takes the dry inside line and passes the wreck at about 40 km/h. Run with the same physics but no warning, car B crashes into the same wall — showing concretely that an early warning saves the following car.

## How realistic is the Circuit Guard physics?
Circuit Guard's wet-hairpin demo, written by Rayyan Moosani, uses a planar dynamic bicycle model integrated at 240 Hz: Pacejka-style tyre forces with a friction ellipse, wheels that lock when braking exceeds grip, aerodynamic downforce and drag, weight transfer, per-side water grip and drag that create split-μ braking and the spin, a driver model that backs off when the front tyres lock, and a spring-damper tyre barrier. It is covered by unit tests that check the slip, the spin before impact, the sideways hit, and that car B survives only when it is warned.

## How does Circuit Guard help with insurance?
Between races, Circuit Guard turns the same data into evidence for a circuit and its insurer. It places every incident on the track zone where it happened and shows serious incidents, marshal call-outs, and which corners sit next to a grandstand. At Montreal, 62% of serious incidents happen in corners next to grandstands, which cover only 25% of the lap. It adds a low-cost safety plan with sourced prices (crews closer, marshal refresher training, standing areas moved back, cameras, guardrail) and an insurer report with the year-by-year trend, so a circuit can bring evidence to its renewal instead of accepting a guess.

## Does Circuit Guard's risk prediction work?
Yes, on real data. Tested on four seasons (2023–2026) of Formula 1 race-control data from Monza, the three riskiest corners from earlier years caught 35 of the next years' 75 serious incidents (47%), where picking three of eleven zones at random would catch about 27% — 1.7 times better than chance. At Montreal the same backtest caught 42 of 83 serious incidents (51%), 2.2 times chance. This is the predictive core of Rayyan Moosani's FormulaTechHacks-winning project.

## What data does Circuit Guard use?
Circuit Guard uses only real or clearly labelled data: OpenF1 race-control messages and telemetry for 2023–2026 (20 sessions per circuit; 268 incidents at Monza and 335 at Montreal), MultiViewer turn and marshal-sector positions, OpenStreetMap grandstands, buildings and barriers, and the FIA 2026 Formula 1 Sporting Regulations. Incident windows are cached offline so the demo runs with no network. Nothing in the live replays is simulated — every car moves on its real recorded position; only the wet-hairpin demo is a (clearly labelled) simulation.

## What is Circuit Guard built with?
Circuit Guard's backend is Python 3.12 with FastAPI, Pydantic v2, NumPy and SciPy, streaming live events over server-sent events. The frontend is Next.js 16 with React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS 4, TanStack Query and Zustand, with a three.js and React Three Fiber 3D digital twin and an SVG fallback. AI: Claude via the Anthropic SDK with structured outputs for the steward cards, Hugging Face Whisper plus an emotion classifier for team-radio severity, and MiniLM (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) embeddings for rulebook retrieval. The Web Audio API synthesises the engine and crash sound. It has 90 backend tests (pytest) and 57 frontend tests (Vitest), and about 26,700 lines of Python and TypeScript across 67 commits and 19 merged pull requests.

## What were the hardest parts of building Circuit Guard?
As team lead, Rayyan Moosani's hardest problems were making real data trustworthy and making the simulation honest. Public telemetry arrives at about 3.7 Hz, where a spin and hard braking look alike, so Circuit Guard judges grip against other cars at the same 100 m of track rather than a fixed threshold, and filters one-sample speed dips as glitches. A simple slip rule flagged normal braking as a crash, so it was replaced with event-based scoring against peers and validated on known incidents. Keeping the AI steward grounded required retrieval, validated schemas, citation checks, and a rule-engine fallback. Making the simulated car spin realistically required modelling each side of the car separately, so water drag plus split-μ braking produce the sideways snap real aquaplaning cars show. OpenF1's free tier allows 30 requests a minute, so every incident window is cached offline for the demo. What Rayyan took away: vehicle dynamics and tyre modelling, designing LLM agents that stay grounded, real-time streaming UIs with server-sent events, 3D visualisation with React Three Fiber, and how motorsport safety, race control and insurance connect in practice.
